There's a difference between compliance and integrity. Compliance is doing what you're required to do. Integrity is doing what you should do regardless of obligation. Most product teams I know have spent the last few years optimizing for compliance, and calling it user experience.

The GDPR was implemented in 2018. In eight years, what most products have learned is how to hide the "reject all" button behind three clicks. That's not design. It's a trap with legal documentation.

<PullQuote>
  "Consent that requires effort to deny is not consent — it's pressure with a friendly interface."
</PullQuote>

## What informed consent actually requires

In medicine, informed consent has specific criteria: the patient needs to understand what they're agreeing to, the available alternatives, and the consequences of each choice. This must be communicated in a way the patient can actually comprehend, not just receive.

Digital product has never taken this seriously. The product version of "informed consent" is a 3,000-word document nobody will read followed by an "Accept" button. The legal team signs off. The product team moves on.

### The structural problem

When the business model depends on behavioral data, genuine consent is adversarial to the business. You can't simultaneously maximize opt-in and ensure the user actually understood what they're giving up. These goals conflict. Most teams resolve this conflict silently in favor of the business and call it a "UX decision."

That's a product decision with ethical consequences. It deserves to be treated as such, not outsourced to the legal team.

## What to do differently

I'm not arguing that every company should implement a consent process that reduces opt-in. I'm arguing that the trade-off needs to be named explicitly internally — and that product teams who've never had that conversation are making an ethical decision without realizing they're making one.

The question that needs to be asked at design time is not "how do we get this accepted?". It's "will the user who accepts this understand what they're accepting?" If the answer is no, you have a product problem, not a copywriting problem.
